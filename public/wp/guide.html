It’s not your fault you’re underprotected — the industry sold you a product instead of a system. Nobody handed you a file when the crew drove away. Nobody told you what an NOA was or why you’d want yours.

The fix starts with understanding one distinction. What you bought is physical — aluminum, glass, anchors. What protects your family after the storm is evidentiary — the approval, the permit, the inspection record, the labels. The physical part works during the storm; the paperwork works during the inspection, the claim review, and the sale of your home — every moment someone who wasn’t there asks, “Can you show me?” Most Florida homeowners own the first part and have never seen the second. This guide closes that gap.

Part One

The five documents that matter

If your home protection had a file folder, here’s what would be in it — and where to find each piece.

1The product approval (NOA or FL number)

Every legitimate impact product in Florida carries a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance (NOA) or a Florida Product Approval (“FL number”) — the proof it was tested to a standard, not just sold with a promise. No approval number, no rating, as far as any verifier is concerned.

Where to look: product labels, your permit, or installer paperwork. FL numbers are searchable at floridabuilding.org; NOAs at miamidade.gov.

2The permit and final inspection

Opening protection is permitted work in Florida. The permit proves it was done legally; the passed final inspection proves a building official signed off on how. No permit means no record the work exists.

Where to look: your county or city building department website — most let you search permits by address, free.

3The wind-mitigation report (Form OIR-B1-1802)

The standardized form a licensed inspector fills out on your home’s wind-resistant features — including opening protection. It’s how insurers understand what your home actually has, and it’s generally valid for five years.

Where to look: your closing documents, your email, or your insurance agent. Check the date — older than five years is expired.

4Labels and photographs

Impact windows carry etched markings in a corner of the glass; shutters carry stamped or affixed labels. These are what an inspector physically verifies — and photos of them survive even when the labels don’t.

Where to look: glass corners, shutter panels, tracks, and housings. Photograph everything you find, today.

5Warranty registration and installer records

Who installed it, when, under what warranty, and whether it transfers. If your installer has disappeared — and in this industry, many have — these records may be the only proof of where your protection came from.

Where to look: your closing folder, installer invoices, manufacturer warranty registration.

Five documents. That’s the whole file. Most homeowners we meet have one, sometimes none.

Part Two

What the inspector is actually looking for

When an inspector evaluates your opening protection, the question is never “does this house have shutters?” It’s “can I verify that every opening is protected by a rated product?” Three rules follow from that:

Evidence beats appearance. A shutter with no label and no paperwork can’t be verified, no matter how sturdy it looks. On the form, “unverifiable” and “unprotected” end up in the same place.

It’s an all-openings standard. Every window, sliding door, and glass entry door counts. One bare bathroom window or garage side door, and the whole home is classified by that weakest point.

The form doesn’t record intentions. It records what can be documented on inspection day — which is why genuinely protected homes can still end up with reports that say otherwise.

We’re a window and door company, not an insurance advisor — what any of this means for your policy is between you and your carrier. What I can tell you, from twenty years of Florida installs, is which features tend to qualify for wind-mitigation documentation and which gaps tend to show up on that form.

Part Three

Why shutter owners get hit hardest

Shutters were sold as a product, not a system. A crew installed panels, collected a check, and left — and here’s what typically didn’t happen:

  • No approval documents were handed over — the NOA existed somewhere, but nobody gave you a copy.
  • Labels weren’t preserved — panels stacked in a garage for fifteen years fade, peel, and get painted over.
  • The approval covers the installation, not just the metal — swapped hardware or off-spec anchoring means the product no longer matches the rating on paper.
  • Some installs were never permitted — no permit, no inspection record, no evidence the work exists.
  • Coverage was partial — front windows shuttered, a side door left bare. Under an all-openings standard, that bare opening defines the home.

None of that is a knock on you — it’s a paperwork norm from an era when nobody thought paperwork was the product. Every one of these gaps is findable, and most are fixable.

Part Four

The ten-minute self-check

Run this tonight. No ladder, no tools, no appointment.

1. Walk the openings.

Count every window and glass door. Flag anything unprotected — including garage side doors and small bathroom windows.

2. Look for markings.

Glass corners on windows; labels on shutter panels, tracks, housings. Photograph everything.

3. Search your permit history.

County building department site, search by address. Look for window/door/shutter permits with passed finals.

4. Find your wind-mitigation report.

Closing documents, email, or your agent. Older than five years = expired.

5. Pull your product paperwork.

Warranties, invoices, anything naming a manufacturer. A product name plus a permit can trace an approval number.

Your file score: ___ of 5 documents in hand.

Five of five? You’re in rare company — just keep the file current. Found gaps? You now know exactly what they are, which puts you ahead of almost every homeowner on your street. Some you can close yourself — permits, photos, a fresh inspection. Others — an unverifiable product, a missing approval, a bare opening — are construction questions, and they deserve a straight answer from someone who can read what you found.
