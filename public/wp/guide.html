The Documented Home Protection Guide

What your paperwork says about your protection — and what to do when it says nothing.

“Protection is not what you install. It’s what you can prove.”

Why I put this guide together

My name is Randy Reece. My father started this company in 1972, and we’ve been protecting Florida homes since 2005. In that time I’ve stood in a lot of living rooms after a lot of storms, and I’ve noticed something that most people in my industry won’t say out loud:

Two homes on the same street can go through the same storm and come out with opposite outcomes. Not because one had better products. Because one had better paperwork.

That’s a hard thing to hear if you’ve already spent real money on shutters or windows. So let me say the second part, because it matters just as much: it’s not your fault you’re underprotected — the industry sold you a product instead of a system.

Nobody handed you a file when the crew drove away. Nobody told you what an NOA was or why you’d want yours. You did what a responsible homeowner does. The category failed you, not the other way around.

This guide fixes the information gap. In the next few pages you’ll learn what documentation actually matters for a Florida home, what a wind-mitigation inspector is looking for when they walk your property, and how to run a ten-minute check on your own house — tonight, for free.

Part One

The gap between “installed” and “proven”

When you bought shutters or windows, you bought two things — you just may not have known it.

The first thing is physical: aluminum, glass, tracks, anchors. That’s the part you can see from the driveway.

The second thing is evidentiary: the product approval that says what you installed was tested and rated, the permit that says it was installed legally, the inspection record that says it was installed correctly, and the labels and photos that let a stranger verify all of it years later.

The physical part protects your home during the storm. The evidentiary part protects your family after it — during the inspection, during the claim review, during the sale of your home, during every moment when someone who wasn’t there asks, “Can you show me?”

Most Florida homeowners own the first part and have never seen the second. That’s the gap this guide closes.

Part Two

The five documents that matter

If your home protection had a file folder, here’s what would be in it.

1The product approval (NOA or FL number)

Every legitimate impact product installed in Florida carries an approval: a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance (NOA) or a Florida Product Approval (an “FL number”). This is the document that proves the product was tested to a standard — not just sold with a promise. It’s usually referenced on a label on the product itself, on the permit, or in the installer’s paperwork. If nobody can produce the approval number for what’s on your home, then as far as any verifier is concerned, it’s unrated.

2The permit and final inspection

Opening protection is permitted work in Florida. The permit record shows the work was done legally; the final inspection record shows a building official signed off on how it was installed. Both live at your county or city building department, and most Florida counties let you search them online by address. Work done without a permit isn’t just a code problem — it’s a documentation dead end.

3The wind-mitigation inspection report (Form OIR-B1-1802)

This is the standardized form a licensed inspector fills out when they evaluate your home’s wind-resistant features — roof shape, roof attachment, and critically for our purposes, opening protection. It’s the document insurers use to understand what your home actually has. These reports are generally valid for five years. If yours is older than that, or you’ve never had one, your home’s protection story is being told by an outdated document — or by no document at all.

4Labels and photographs

Impact windows carry permanent markings, usually etched in a corner of the glass. Shutters carry stamped or affixed labels. These are what an inspector physically looks for. Photographs of labels, of installed hardware, and of anchoring details give you a record that survives even if a label fades, a shutter panel gets repainted, or a document gets lost.

5Warranty registration and installer records

Who installed it, when, under what warranty, and whether that warranty transfers. If the company that installed your protection has disappeared — and in this industry, many have — these records may be the only proof of the installation’s provenance.

Five documents. That’s the whole file. Most homeowners we meet have one of them, sometimes none.

Part Three

What the wind-mitigation inspector is actually looking for

Here’s the part almost nobody explains to homeowners before the inspector shows up.

When an inspector evaluates your opening protection, they are not asking, “Does this house have shutters?” They’re asking, “Can I verify that every opening on this house is protected by a rated product?”

That distinction changes everything, for three reasons:

Reason one: verification requires evidence, not appearance. The inspector needs to see a label, a marking, a product approval number, or permit documentation that ties what’s on your home to a tested rating. A shutter with no label and no paperwork can’t be verified — no matter how sturdy it looks. On the form, “unverifiable” and “unprotected” end up in the same place.

Reason two: it’s an all-openings standard. The classification looks at every glazed opening — every window, every sliding door, every entry door with glass. The strongest classifications require all of them to be verifiably protected. One skipped bathroom window, one garage side door, one opening the original crew ran out of panels for, and the whole home is classified by that weakest point.

Reason three: the form doesn’t record intentions. It records what can be documented on inspection day. Whatever your protection actually is, the report only reflects what you can prove — which is why homeowners with genuinely protected homes can still end up with reports that say otherwise.

We’re a window and door company, not an insurance advisor, so I won’t tell you what any of this means for your policy — that’s between you and your carrier. What I can tell you, from twenty years of Florida installs, is which features tend to qualify for wind-mitigation documentation and which gaps tend to show up on that form. That’s a construction question, and it’s one we can answer.

Part Four

Why shutter owners have this problem more than anyone

If you have shutters, the honest odds are that some of what you just read applies to your home. That’s not a knock on you. It’s how the shutter category was sold.

Shutters were marketed as a product, not a system. A crew installed panels or accordions, collected a check, and left. Here’s what typically didn’t happen:

  • No approval documents were handed over. The NOA existed somewhere — at the manufacturer, maybe in a permit file — but nobody gave you a copy or told you it mattered.
  • Labels weren’t preserved. Panel shutters get stacked in a garage for fifteen years. Labels fade, peel, get painted over. Accordion housings get caulked and repainted. The physical evidence erodes.
  • The approval covers the installation, not just the metal. An NOA specifies anchors, spacing, and mounting details. If hardware was swapped over the years, or the original anchoring never matched the approval, the product on your wall may no longer match the rating on paper.
  • Some installs were never permitted at all. Especially older ones. No permit means no inspection record, which means the county has no evidence the work exists.
  • Coverage was partial. It was common to shutter the big front windows and leave a side door or a small bathroom window bare. Under an all-openings standard, that’s the opening that defines the home.

So when I say most shutter owners have never seen their NOA, I’m not trying to scare you. I’m describing a paperwork norm from an era when nobody thought paperwork was the product. The good news: every one of these gaps is findable, and most are fixable.

Part Five

The ten-minute self-check

You can run this tonight. No ladder, no tools, no appointment.

1. Walk the openings.

Count every window and glass door. Note any opening with no protection at all — that includes garage side doors and small bathroom windows. Weakest opening defines the home.

2. Look for markings.

On impact windows, check the corners of the glass for etched markings. On shutters, look for stamped or affixed labels on panels, tracks, or housings. Photograph everything you find.

3. Search your permit history.

Go to your county or city building department website and search permits by your address. Look for window, door, or shutter permits — and whether each shows a passed final inspection.

4. Find your wind-mitigation report.

Check your closing documents, your email, or ask whoever insured the home. Note the date — reports older than five years are generally expired.

5. Pull your product paperwork.

Warranty documents, installer invoices, anything naming a manufacturer or product line. A product name plus a permit is often enough to trace an approval number.

6. Score yourself honestly.

Five documents from Part Two. How many can you put your hands on right now?

If you found all five: genuinely, well done — you’re in rare company, and your job is just to keep that file current.

If you found gaps: you now know exactly what they are, which puts you ahead of almost every homeowner on your street. The question is just what to do next.

Part Six

What to do with what you found

Some gaps you can close yourself: pulling permit records, photographing labels, scheduling an updated wind-mitigation inspection with a licensed inspector.

Some gaps are harder: an unverifiable product, a missing approval, an opening that was never protected, an install that never matched its rating. Those are construction questions, and they deserve a straight answer from someone who can actually read what you found.
